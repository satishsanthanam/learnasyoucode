Executive Summary

The regions now known as Pakistan and Bangladesh did not exist as sovereign states during the period 1600–1947. Before the partition of August 1947, they were integral territories of British India: the present-day Islamic Republic of Pakistan corresponds to the western provinces of Sindh, Punjab, the North-West Frontier, and Balochistan, while the present-day People's Republic of Bangladesh corresponds to the eastern portion of the Bengal province, historically called East Bengal. Because the freedom struggle was waged across the whole of British India, the inhabitants of these regions participated in, shaped, and were shaped by, the movement for independence, and the principal organizational vehicle of that movement was the Indian National Congress (INC), founded in 1885.

Drawing on the authoritative reference content on the Indian National Congress, this report identifies several pivotal contributions anchored in the territories that now form Pakistan and Bangladesh:

  • East Bengal (now Bangladesh) and the Swadeshi Movement (1905–1911): The partition of Bengal in 1905, which divided the historic province including the territory of modern Bangladesh, triggered the Swadeshi Movement. This was the first organized mass collective action in the struggle for independence, employing the boycott of British imports and the promotion of Indian-made goods under the principle of economic self-sufficiency.
  • Punjab and the Jallianwala Bagh massacre (1919): The massacre of unarmed civilians at Jallianwala Bagh in Amritsar, Punjab—a province subsequently divided between India and Pakistan in 1947—produced overwhelming outrage across India. Together with the perceived feebleness of the Rowlatt Act reforms, this event propelled the Congress Party under Mahatma Gandhi into sustained nonviolent civil disobedience.
  • Lahore (now in Pakistan) and the Purna Swaraj declaration (1929): The Congress session held at Lahore in December 1929 marked the culmination of the party's constitutional evolution. There, the Congress formally adopted Purna Swaraj ("Complete Self-Rule") as its goal, superseding the dominion status demanded in the Nehru Report of 1928. The declaration was made public on January 26, 1930.

The report further traces how these regional anchors were integrated into the all-India movement through the Congress Party's nationwide campaigns, culminating in independence in August 1947. That independence was accompanied by the partition of the subcontinent into a Hindu-majority India and a Muslim-majority Pakistan, with the eastern wing of Pakistan—carved from East Bengal—later becoming Bangladesh in 1971.

1. Introduction

1.1 The Research Question

The research topic under examination is: "Role of Pakistan and Bangladesh regions in Indian freedom struggle (1600–1947)." The description clarifies that the research focuses on the historical contribution of the territories that now constitute Pakistan and Bangladesh—as they existed before 1947—to India's independence movement against British rule. The timeframe spans from 1600 to 1947, with an emphasis on support for freedom from English colonial rule. Events after 1947 and after 1971 are explicitly excluded from the scope of inquiry.

1.2 The Temporal and Conceptual Challenge

A significant conceptual challenge must be addressed at the outset. The terms "Pakistan" and "Bangladesh" are modern political designations. Pakistan was created in 1947 through the partition of British India. Bangladesh emerged in 1971 from the eastern wing of Pakistan, which had previously been the eastern portion of Bengal. To speak of "Pakistan and Bangladesh regions" during 1600–1947 is therefore to refer, in a retrospective idiom, to territories that were administratively and culturally part of British India.

The relevant territorial identifications are as follows: the present-day Islamic Republic of Pakistan corresponds broadly to the western provinces and regions of British India—Punjab, Sindh, the North-West Frontier, and Balochistan. The present-day People's Republic of Bangladesh corresponds to East Bengal, the eastern portion of the Bengal province of British India. This report uses the modern designations only as a practical convenience to connect historical developments to the present question, while remaining explicit about the historical administrative geography and flagging the anachronism inherent in the research framing.

2. Historical Background: Anti-Colonial Thought and Antecedents (1600–1885)

2.1 The East India Company Era

According to the source, anti-colonial thought in India can be traced back to the East India Company's political and commercial activities in the 18th century. This is the earliest period covered by the available material within the research timeline. During this era, the Company's expanding commercial enterprise established the structural conditions for later colonial exploitation and, eventually, for a growing anti-colonial consciousness. The source notes that anti-colonial thought "intensified in the mid-19th century," a development closely connected with the consolidation of British rule and the economic and political changes that accompanied it.

2.2 The Indian Rebellion of 1857 and the British Raj

The source indicates that the roots of the Indian National Congress lie in the early Indian nationalist movement that arose from the Indian Rebellion of 1857. The rebellion was a watershed in the history of the subcontinent: it ended the East India Company's rule and led to the establishment of the British raj—the direct rule of India by the British Crown. It was in the aftermath of this transition that organized nationalist movements began to take institutional form.

2.3 The Indian Association and Other Precursors

After the establishment of the British raj, organized nationalist movements were formed to advance the cause of greater participation by Indians in administrative affairs. The source names one such movement: the Indian Association. These organizations are described as "precursors of the Indian National Congress."

3. The Founding of the Indian National Congress and Its All-India Character (1885)

3.1 The Founding of 1885

The Indian National Congress was founded in 1885, initially with the goal of pursuing moderate reform under the British raj. It was founded by several Indian nationalist leaders, such as Dadabhai Naoroji and Surendranath Banerjee, as well as Allan Octavian Hume, a British official in the Indian civil service. The Congress Party first convened in December 1885 in Bombay (now Mumbai), with 72 members in attendance and W.C. Bonnerjee as president.

3.2 The Congress as an All-India Vehicle

From 1885 onward, the freedom struggle had a single, broadly based organizational vehicle—the Indian National Congress—within which the political energies of all regions, including those now in Pakistan and Bangladesh, were channeled. Congress sessions rotated among major cities across the subcontinent—Bombay, Lahore, Surat, and Amritsar—ensuring that participants from all provinces were brought together in a shared political project.

4. The Moderate Years: The Constitutional Phase (1885–1905)

4.1 Goals and Methods of the Moderates

During its first several decades, the Congress passed fairly moderate reform resolutions. The party's initial goal was to achieve constitutional changes within the colonial framework. Its methods relied on meetings, petitions, and press campaigns to press its demands upon the British administration.

4.2 Radicalization Through Economic Critique

Even as the party pursued moderate methods, many of its members were being radicalized by the increased poverty that accompanied British imperialism. The moderates were "extraordinarily successful" in constructing a damaging economic critique of colonialism based on the socioeconomic conditions of 19th-century India: "Exploitative British policies combined with famines and epidemics impoverished India, which had been reduced to a supplier of raw materials to England and a consumer of the imported finished goods."

5. The Bengal Region (Modern Bangladesh) and the Swadeshi Movement (1905–1911)

5.1 The Partition of Bengal

The Congress Party began to transform into a nationwide movement in response to the partition of Bengal (1905–11). The partition divided the historic Bengal province, creating an eastern portion—East Bengal—that corresponds geographically to the territory of present-day Bangladesh.

5.2 Emergence of Extremists & Swadeshi

An "extremist" faction emerged within the Congress Party, consisting of the "Lal Bal Pal" trio—Lala Lajpat Rai, Bal Gangadhar Tilak, and Bipin Chandra Pal—alongside Annie Besant. This faction endorsed swadeshi ("of our own country"), calling on Indians to boycott imported British goods and promote Indian-made goods.

5.3 First Organized Mass Collective Action

The Swadeshi Movement, launched in 1905, became the first organized mass collective action in the struggle for independence. Garnering the support of a wide swath of social classes, it centered in Bengal (including East Bengal/Bangladesh) and served as the template for future mass movements.

6. The Surat Split and the Radicalization of the Movement (1907–1917)

Disagreements between extremists and moderates (led by Gopal Krishna Gokhale) culminated in an acrimonious session at Surat in 1907. Chaos ensued when the extremists demanded Tilak speak before president-elect Rash Behari Ghose; shoes and chairs were thrown, suspending the session. Factions reconciled at the 1915 Bombay session. By 1917, Annie Besant (who started the Home Rule League in 1916) became the party's first woman president, reflecting the ascendancy of mass-oriented politics across the subcontinent.

7. Punjab, Lahore, and the Rise of Gandhian Civil Disobedience (1919–1930)

7.1 Jallianwala Bagh and Strategy Shift

In 1919, dissatisfaction with the Rowlatt Act reforms and outrage over the Jallianwala Bagh massacre in Amritsar, Punjab propelled Gandhi and the Congress into nonviolent noncooperation. Punjab—a province partitioned in 1947 between India and Pakistan—was the catalyst for this monumental strategy shift.

7.2 Civil Disobedience Campaigns

The All India Congress Committee, formed in 1929, coordinated civil disobedience including tax avoidance. In 1930, Gandhi led the landmark Salt March accompanied by leaders like Sarojini Naidu, inaugurating a nationwide satyagraha.

8. The Demand for Purna Swaraj: The Lahore Session of 1929

Revising the 1928 Nehru Report's demand for dominion status, the historic Congress session held at Lahore (now in Pakistan) in December 1929 formally adopted Purna Swaraj ("Complete Self-Rule"). Made public on January 26, 1930, this declaration established total independence as the unyielding goal of the freedom struggle.

9. The Swaraj Party and Electoral Politics (1923–1937)

Led by Motilal Nehru and Chittaranjan Das, the Swaraj Party contested elections within the colonial framework. In the 1937 elections, the Swaraj Party won 7 out of 11 provinces across British India, demonstrating sweeping popular electoral backing for nationalist governance.

10. World War II and the Quit India Movement (1939–1945)

When Britain unilaterally declared India a belligerent in WWII in 1939, Congress refused support without complete independence. In 1942, Congress launched the mass Quit India Movement. British authorities imprisoned the entire Congress leadership (including Gandhi) until 1945, triggering widespread subcontinental unrest.

11. Independence and the Partition of the Subcontinent (1945–1947)

Following WWII, Clement Attlee's Labour government resolved to exit India. Parliament passed the Independence Bill in July 1947, leading to independence in August 1947. The subcontinent was partitioned into a Hindu-majority India and a Muslim-majority Pakistan (encompassing West Pakistan and East Bengal/East Pakistan).

16. Conclusion and Implications

16.1 Principal Conclusions

  1. Integral Participation: The territories of modern Pakistan and Bangladesh were core constituent parts of British India, participating fully in the subcontinental independence movement.
  2. Bangladesh as Mass Epicenter: East Bengal hosted the Swadeshi Movement (1905), the first organized mass collective action of the freedom struggle.
  3. Pakistan as Strategic Turning Point: Punjab and Lahore hosted the Jallianwala Bagh massacre (1919) and the Purna Swaraj declaration (1929)—the two most consequential strategic turning points of the movement.
  4. Unified Heritage: Partition in 1947 was a political division at decolonization, not a reflection of separate participation in the struggle against British rule.
